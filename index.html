What am I seeing?

NISAR is a radar satellite built by NASA and ISRO. Radar works at night and through clouds, so it can measure the same ground again and again.

Each date shows cumulative relative line-of-sight (LOS) change since 20 Jun 2026. LOS is the satellite viewing direction, not a direct measurement of vertical movement. The radar wavelength is about 24 cm.

  • Red and blue mean change in opposite directions. Which one is toward the satellite is not confirmed yet.
  • Transparent areas are masked because they do not have a reliable measurement. Transparency does not mean zero change.
  • Click the map to see how one spot changed over time.

This map does not prove that the ground is sinking or rising. The apparent signal is comparable to the estimated noise and needs independent validation.

Method and limitations

Data. NISAR L2 GUNW (Provisional), track 92, frame 73, descending, L-band, HH. Nine consecutive pairs from 20 Jun to 18 Oct 2026, downloaded from the ASF DAAC. One pair spans 24 days, the others 12.

Steps. Pixels with coherence of 0.5 or less, or outside the main unwrapped area, are masked. The estimated ionospheric phase is subtracted. Each pair is referenced to the median of its valid pixels within the Quetta study region. Phase is converted to centimeters (about 1.94 cm per radian) and the pairs are added up over time.

  • The data is Provisional and not fully validated.
  • Roughly 2–3 cm of noise remains in areas that are probably stable.
  • Troposphere and solid Earth tide corrections are not applied.
  • The reference is a regional median, not a confirmed stable point.
  • Only about 57% of pixels survive the masks by the last date.
  • One satellite track gives line-of-sight change only, not vertical or horizontal movement.
  • Four months is a short record, and no independently validated stable reference area was established.
