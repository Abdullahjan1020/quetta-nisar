Quetta valley, seen by radar

A NASA–ISRO satellite measures tiny changes in the distance between itself and the ground. This map shows how those measurements changed between June and October 2026.

  • Play or drag through the timeline
  • Hover to read a value, click to see one spot over time
  • Switch to Satellite to see the land underneath

Early, provisional data: explore it, do not take it as proof of ground movement.

What am I seeing?

NISAR is a radar satellite built by NASA and ISRO. Radar works at night and through clouds, so it can measure the same ground again and again.

Each date shows how much the distance between the satellite and the ground has changed since 20 Jun 2026, measured along the satellite’s line of sight. A few centimeters is a small fraction of the radar wavelength (about 24 cm).

  • Red and blue mean change in opposite directions. Which one is toward the satellite is not confirmed yet.
  • Transparent areas have no reliable measurement, or almost no change.

This map does not prove that the ground is sinking or rising. It shows where a signal might be worth a closer look.

Method and limitations

Data. NISAR L2 GUNW (Provisional), track 92, frame 73, descending, L-band, HH. Nine consecutive pairs from 20 Jun to 18 Oct 2026 (ASF DAAC). One pair spans 24 days, the others 12.

Steps. Pixels with coherence of 0.5 or less are masked. The ionospheric phase is subtracted. Each pair is shifted by the median of its valid pixels. Phase becomes centimeters (about 1.94 cm per radian) and pairs are added up over time.

  • Provisional data, not fully validated; roughly 2–3 cm of noise remains.
  • No troposphere or solid Earth tide correction; the reference is a regional median.
  • One satellite track: line-of-sight change only. Only about 57% of pixels survive by the last date.
